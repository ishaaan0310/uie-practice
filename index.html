Tackling a new question

  1. “All of the above” is usually right. In the 130 course questions it was correct 10 times out of 11. If it's an option and at least two others look true, pick it.
  2. Cross out extreme or negative options. Answers with “only”, “solely”, “ignore”, “avoid”, “without” or “disregard” were correct just 2 times out of 125. About 1 in 5 wrong options use them.
  3. Pick the customer. When unsure, choose the option about understanding customer needs, getting feedback, validating or iterating. That is the course's core message.
  4. Balanced beats one-sided. Options that balance users, technology and business (desirability, feasibility, viability) beat ones that chase a single goal like profit or looks.
  5. Prefer the specific, sensible option. The longest, most precise option was correct about half the time, twice what guessing gives.
  6. Multi-answer questions: the app tells you how many to pick. Choose every option that is reasonable and positive, and drop the extreme ones.
Week 0 · Business basics
  • Business structures: sole proprietorship (one owner, unlimited liability), partnership (2+ owners share profit and responsibility), company/corporation (separate legal entity). A trademark is IP, not a structure.
  • IPO = Initial Public Offering. KPI = Key Performance Indicator. SWOT = Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, Threats.
  • Unicorn = private startup valued over $1 billion (OYO, Swiggy).
  • Entrepreneurship = starting and running a venture that solves a problem; needs a target market, business plan and funding.
Week 1 · Ideas, disruption and Lean Startup
  • Lean Startup (Eric Ries): Build → Measure → Learn, minimise time through the loop, validated learning, pivot or persevere.
  • Disruptive innovation (Clayton Christensen): changes that challenge market norms, often starting cheaper or simpler. Schumpeter: entrepreneurs cause “creative destruction”.
  • Invention = new idea or device. Innovation = making it useful and adopted.
  • Failure still has value: lessons and insights. Common mistakes: building without customers, focusing on product over market.
  • Evidence-based entrepreneurship = decisions from data and experiments, not assumptions.
Week 2 · Founders, teams and early adopters
  • Top reason startups fail: no real market need. Funding does not guarantee success.
  • Customers validate a product, not founders or investors. Early adopters give critical feedback.
  • Strong teams: shared vision, complementary skills, communication, trust. Founders need strong belief in the vision.
  • When progress stalls: re-evaluate and pivot.
  • Burn rate = cash spent per month. Runway = months until cash runs out. Churn = customers who leave. Premature scaling = growing before product–market fit.
Week 3 · Design, teams and patents
  • Empathy leads to user-centred design. Ideal team = diverse skills and perspectives, low ego, flexible.
  • Design brief: functional and user requirements, pain points, user journeys. Problem statement: define the problem clearly, not the solution.
  • Regulations protect public health and ensure legal compliance.
  • Prior art = earlier patents and publications. Patent tests: novelty, inventive step (non-obvious), industrial use. Validity about 20 years. Provisional filing secures an early date. FTO checks you don't infringe others.
Week 4 · Customers and markets
  • Outside-in thinking = start from customer needs. Inside-out = start from your own tech.
  • Problem recognition scale: latent (unaware) → passive (aware, no motivation to change) → active/urgent (actively seeking a solution). Target active or urgent first.
  • Rural and sustainable products: government schemes, demonstrations, communities, local partners, affordable pricing.
  • Diffusion of Innovation: innovators → early adopters → early majority → late majority → laggards. The chasm sits between early adopters and early majority.
  • Investors want a real, solvable problem. New markets need cultural and economic understanding.
Week 5 · From lab to market
  • TRL (Technology Readiness Levels) 1–9: TRL 1 basic principles, TRL 4 lab validation, TRL 6 prototype in a relevant environment, TRL 9 proven in real operation.
  • Order: Proof of Concept → Prototype → Pilot production → Mass production. POC proves functional feasibility. Pilot runs test and adapt the design with users.
  • Secure IPR before commercialising. Anyone can license IPR: startups, industry, NGOs.
  • Healthcare startups: low device success rates, long clinical and regulatory timelines (CDSCO in India). Telemedicine eased hospital load in pandemics.
  • Technology transfer = moving lab research to industry, best done by partnering with industry.
Week 6 · Design research and strategy
  • Human-centred design: empathy and user research. Three lenses: desirability (people want it), feasibility (can be built), viability (makes business sense).
  • Interviews: build rapport and trust, ask open-ended questions about real past behaviour.
  • Tools: personas, empathy maps (say/think/do/feel), user journey maps, Five Whys for root cause, ethnography (observe in context).
  • Design management = a structured process to meet business goals through design. Clients want quality, speed and transparency.
Week 7 · Design thinking and business models
  • Design thinking: empathise → define → ideate → prototype → test. It is collaborative, iterative and non-linear.
  • Synectics = creativity by connecting unrelated ideas. Three critical factors: market need, technical feasibility, business viability.
  • Business Model Canvas (Osterwalder), 9 blocks: customer segments, value propositions, channels, customer relationships, revenue streams, key resources, key activities, key partners, cost structure.
  • Lean Canvas (Ash Maurya) adds problem, solution, key metrics and unfair advantage. It is used to test assumptions fast.
  • Models: fractional ownership (shared physical assets), subscription, freemium, collaborative. Double whammy = two bad things at once.
Week 8 · MVP, pitching and IP
  • MVP attracts early adopters and validates the idea with real customers. A prototype mainly tests design internally.
  • Lean Canvas steps: target customers → top 3 problems → solutions.
  • Pitch essentials: a scalable, profitable business model, clear value proposition. Elevator pitch is 30–60 seconds.
  • IP types: patent (inventions), copyright (creative expression), trademark (brand name or logo), trade secret (confidential know-how, no registration), industrial design (appearance).
  • Metrics: CAC = customer acquisition cost. LTV should be much greater than CAC. Acquisition strategy aims for long-term retention. A strong UVP attracts and keeps customers.
Week 9 · Funding and incubation
  • Barriers to action: low confidence, fear of feedback, funding. The most important relationship is with target customers.
  • Early funding: government grants, seed funding, crowdfunding, angel investors. Order: seed → Series A → B → C → IPO. VCs take equity, which dilutes founders.
  • Incubation stage = building the business plan and securing resources. Accelerators run short cohort programmes for fast growth.
  • Entrepreneur sees problems as business opportunities. Innovator focuses on solving the problem.
  • Commercialisation = taking an idea to industry through IP or tech transfer. Clinical immersion finds unmet healthcare needs. Startup India gives recognition and tax benefits.
Week 10 · Creativity and prototyping
  • Synectics analogies: direct (compare with nature, e.g. Velcro from burrs), personal (become the problem), symbolic (poetic, abstract image), fantasy (ideal or impossible solutions). Goal: break conventional thinking.
  • Prototyping methods: additive (3D printing), subtractive (CNC, laser cutting), forming (moulding, vacuum forming). Lateral thinking is not a method.
  • Development sequence: ideation → concept generation → prototyping → testing → market launch.
  • Reasoning: inductive (specific → general), deductive (general → specific), abductive (best guess from incomplete info, used in design).
  • Frugal innovation reduces waste and cost. Prototype early to find flaws.
Week 11 · Inclusive products and risk
  • For specially-abled users: user-centred design. Assistive tech should make users independent and socially included.
  • Success factors: relevant and differentiated. Startups should offer a unique product, not unrealistic promises.
  • Avoid cheap materials in B2C: returns and servicing cost more.
  • Risk: depending on a few corporate clients. B2B sales involve many stakeholders and longer cycles.
  • Human interaction builds empathy that AI lacks. Evidence-based products measure impact on target users. Scalability = revenue grows faster than costs.
Week 12 · Launch, marketing and pitching
  • Identify problems early to give development a clear direction. Modular, customisable products raise satisfaction and efficiency.
  • Beachhead market = one focused segment to win first, then expand to adjacent segments.
  • Startup marketing: innovative and lean, networking through targeted connections, word of mouth. Inverted pyramid model puts customer needs first.
  • Most crucial design step: identify the target customer segment.
  • Pitch: problem, solution, market size (TAM–SAM–SOM), business model, traction, team, financials, the ask. Explain clearly how the product solves the problem.
